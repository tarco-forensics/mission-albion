Forensic Constituency Analysis and Local Dynamics

Each of the three constituencies harbours its own distinct demographic constraints and psychosocial crisis dynamics. These dynamics bear directly upon voter reflexes.

A. Makerfield: The Contest Between Tribal Threat and Collective Nostalgia

Under ordinary conditions, Makerfield — with its low university graduation rate (18.3%) and structural economic stagnation — would be among the constituencies most susceptible to the populist right's rhetoric of fear and existential threat. When individuals feel insecurity in their daily lives and their faith in institutional justice erodes, they become inclined to retreat to the “tribal shelter” in moments of perceived danger, suspending rational deliberation. Robert Kenyon (Reform UK) was adeptly exploiting this threat perception alongside a deep-seated resentment towards “metropolitan elites.” Kenyon's personal scandals are not read by his core electorate as a liability; on the contrary, they serve as proof of an “anti-system” identity — the mark of someone challenging the established order from the outside.

However, Labour's candidate Andy Burnham has penetrated this wall of fear with a powerful counter-signal: the Channel of Collective Nostalgia and Belonging. Burnham has positioned himself not as a generic Westminster bureaucrat but through the narrative of a “miner's grandson,” his Northern identity, and cultural symbols such as Oasis. This triggers in the voter, beyond rational policy arguments, a sense of shared belonging rooted in place and history — a collective memory frame. These shared memory frames neutralise the external threat perception stoked by the populist right and shape voter preferences accordingly. The fragmentation of right-wing votes by Restore Britain (7.39%) further caps Reform UK's vote ceiling.

B. Aberdeen South: Energy Transition Anxiety and Institutional Decay

In this Scottish seat vacated by former SNP Westminster leader Stephen Flynn, the SNP is on the defensive. The constituency's high educational attainment (45.2%) creates strong cognitive resistance to the populist right's simple survival rhetoric, whilst rendering voters more critically disposed towards the performance of local institutions. The employment uncertainties and loss of local prosperity brought about by the transition to green energy have eroded confidence in SNP governance. In an environment where the sense of institutional justice and belonging has been worn away, voters are reacting by gravitating towards Labour's candidate Hoque Ali, making him the 72.16% favourite.

C. Arbroath and Broughty Ferry: Coastal Economic Stagnation and a Divided Opposition

The fury over fishing quotas and the stagnation of the coastal economy have built up intense structural resentment among voters. Under normal circumstances, this could have produced a punishing surge of votes directed against the SNP, which has been in government. However, examining the current political trends across Scotland, it is clear that the voice of opposition has been unable to coalesce around a single focal point. Trade-unionist and conservative votes are fragmented across Labour, the Conservative Party, and Reform UK. This fragmentation makes it considerably easier for SNP candidate Lara Bird to hold the gap, giving her a comfortable winning margin of 86.73%.

Deep Cognitive Dynamics: Belief Regime and Temporal Collapse

To understand the sociological oscillations underpinning these by-elections, it is necessary to focus on three deep psychosocial concepts that form the foundation of the Seldon-Court simulation model:

1. Belief Regime Pressure and the Fatalistic Buffer

A society's dogmatic belief patterns and levels of institutional deference determine how quickly it reacts against the system in moments of crisis. When belief regime pressure is high, voters respond to economic deterioration or institutional decay not through rational interrogation but with a fatalistic buffer — a passive acceptance that defers challenge to the system indefinitely. Yet this acceptance has limits; once a critical threshold is crossed, the societal reaction erupts in a sudden, sharp phase transition: mass flight from the establishment or a rapid collapse of vote share. The abrupt fracturing of tribal resistance in Makerfield through Burnham's belonging narrative is a concrete instance of precisely such a threshold breach.

2. Temporal Horizon Collapse and Immediate Impulsivity

In individuals living under chronic socio-economic stress and intense media saturation, the capacity for rational planning and long-term benefit analysis within the brain becomes suppressed. As uncertainty about the future increases, a person's sense of time bends inward, contracting into an “immediate narrowing.” The voter whose temporal horizon has collapsed no longer defends their future rational interests; instead, they seek refuge in the instant emotional rewards offered by populist narratives, or they live through selectively remembered traumas of the past as though they were present realities. It is precisely this temporal collapse that consolidates Kenyon's base in Makerfield — while Burnham has turned that very collapse to his own advantage by managing the “nostalgia” channel.

3. Social Decay and the Hoffer Dimensions

According to Eric Hoffer's theory of social dissolution, communal decay begins with the erosion of three foundational pillars: the loss of a sense of public safety, the disappearance of institutional justice, and the collapse of collective hope for the future. Individuals who have become rootless and have lost hope tend to seek shelter in radical or tribal mass movements in order to feel secure. In former industrial heartlands such as Makerfield and Arbroath, the breakdown of institutional justice — a pervasive sense of impunity — combined with the hopelessness of younger generations about their future, has transformed politics from a rational contest of programmes into a tribal struggle for survival.

These three by-elections confirm that the traditional two-party hegemony in the United Kingdom has been permanently eroded. Whilst a Labour victory in Makerfield will produce a degree of temporary relief at the national level, the entrenched vote base of Reform UK and other anti-establishment actors demonstrates that right-wing populism has become a permanent structural force. Voter behaviour is now shaped not merely by economic promises but by the management of fear, nostalgia, and local identity codes.

The boundaries of governability are shifting away from single-party absolute majorities and towards a permanent regime of compromise, fragmentation, and “continuous negotiation.” The United Kingdom's Parliament will be compelled to confront not the question of who “wins,” but how this fractured political landscape can be governed at all.

Politics is no longer a contest between rational programmes; it is a struggle for the management of mass populations through the channels of fear, nostalgia and local identity.
